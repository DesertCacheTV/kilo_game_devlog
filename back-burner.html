Not every idea works the first time, and that's part of the fun. This is where the paused, replaced and removed bits of Kilo Man Jar-O live. Each one has what it was, what happened, and what we learned.

The labels: {% include status.html s="Back burner" %} paused, might come back. {% include status.html s="Replaced" %} swapped for something better. {% include status.html s="Removed" %} taken out. {% include status.html s="Fixed" %} a little oops we caught. New words? Try the glossary.

Paused for now

Flying pickles {% include status.html s="Back burner" %}

What it was
Little pickle cannons along the run that fired pickles at Kilo Man. Each hit cost 1 HP.
What happened
In play-testing, the pickles were too small and too fast to see coming. Getting hit felt random, not fair. The cannons are switched off, not deleted.
What we learned
A danger should be something you can spot and react to. A new hazard, something you can see coming and outrun, is planned instead.

The storm that rolls in {% include status.html s="Back burner" %}

What it was
A storm that slowly darkens the sky and the fog as you run, so the mood changes partway through a level.
What happened
Behind the scenes, the numbers that control the sky really did change. But in a real play-test, after running 600 m, the screen looked exactly the same: no darkening, no storm clouds. So it went on the back burner.
What we learned
"The computer says it works" isn't the same as "you can see it working". Around here, nothing counts as working until it's been played.

The splashy puddle {% include status.html s="Back burner" %}

What it was
A drop of water on the ant trail (a puddle, at Kilo Man's size) with a few pebbles. Stepping in it slowed you down and pushed you back for about a second.
What happened
The splash itself worked, but the puddle didn't sit right on the trail. Its edges didn't match where the water looked like it was, so you could run straight past it, spam jump to hop it, or sometimes clear the whole thing in one leap. It looked awful, so it came out of the trail's mix of pieces and into its own back-burner folder.
What we learned
A hazard is only as good as where it sits. If it can be skipped without trying, it isn't really a hazard. When the puddle comes back, it needs a proper fit on the trail and an edge that matches what you see.

Replaced with something better

The first trail spawner {% include status.html s="Replaced" %}

What it was
The very first system that laid track in front of you, one tile after another.
What happened
After a long run and a retry, it could leave the start of the trail empty. The new trail builder replaced it, and it rebuilds the start properly on every retry.
What we learned
Planning the whole level as a recipe up front made retries simpler and more reliable.

The first sky {% include status.html s="Replaced" %}

What it was
The original sky setup, with its own clouds and a moon.
What happened
A second, better sky took over. The old one is parked and hidden, not deleted, in case we need anything from it.
What we learned
Keep the old version around until the new one has proven itself.

Small oops, caught and fixed

Softer cloud edges {% include status.html s="Removed" %}

What it was
An experiment to fade the edges of the clouds so they'd look softer.
What happened
It made the clouds disappear completely. We rolled it straight back to the version that worked.
What we learned
Save a checkpoint before every experiment, so a bad one only costs a minute.

The tilted camera {% include status.html s="Fixed" %}

What it was
After pressing Retry, the camera came back slightly tilted, like the world was on a slope.
What happened
The start marker had a tiny tilt hidden in it, and Retry copied it. Now Retry only copies which way you're facing, so the view matches a fresh start.
What we learned
Little numbers hide in little places. Test the second run too, not just the first.

Rock scatter {% include status.html s="Removed" %}

What it was
An automatic sprinkle of rocks along the early path pieces.
What happened
It was taken out during a tidy-up of the path pieces. The game played fine without it.
What we learned
Less clutter, fewer things to break. Hand-picked set dressing can come back later.

A rule we're keeping: drag-and-drop weather

Weather in Kilo Man Jar-O isn't painted into the sky. Each kind of weather is its own piece you can drop into any level, like a sticker on a map. That way any level can be sunny, cloudy, stormy or (one day) rainy without being rebuilt.
